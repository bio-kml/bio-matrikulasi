1.1 Biodiversity and classification ​

Learning outcomes
  • (a) State types of biodiversity: genetic, species and ecosystem. C1
  • (b) State hierarchical classification. C1
  • (c) Explain briefly the three-domain system (Carl Woese, 1977): Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya, based on rRNA base sequence. C3
Before you start

You need these ideas from earlier. Tap one if you need a reminder.

  • Prokaryotic cells have no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles; their DNA lies free in the cytoplasm. Eukaryotic cells have a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles such as mitochondria.

  • Ribosomes make proteins. They are built from ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and protein. Every living cell has ribosomes, so comparing rRNA base sequences lets us compare all organisms.

  • A group of organisms that can interbreed in nature and produce fertile offspring.

  • A unicellular organism is a single cell; a multicellular organism is made of many cells.

Biodiversity: the variety of living things, their genes, and the ecosystems they live in.

Types of biodiversity ​

Genetic diversity

Differences in genes within a population and between populations.

Species diversity

The number of species in an ecosystem or in the whole biosphere.

Ecosystem diversity

The variety of ecosystems and ecological processes in the biosphere.

Hierarchical classification ​

Remember the order

Domain → Kingdom → Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species

A taxon is a group at any level, such as a genus or a phylum. Related species go into one genus, related genera into one family, and so on upward.

Binomial nomenclature (Carolus Linnaeus) gives every species a two-part name: Genus + specific epithet. The genus starts with a capital, the epithet is all lowercase, and the whole name is italic when typed or underlined when handwritten. Example: Rana temporaria (frog), shortened to R. temporaria.

1.2 Domain Bacteria and Domain Archaea ​

Learning outcomes
  • (a) State the two domains of prokaryotes: Bacteria (e.g. E. coli) and Archaea (e.g. Sulfolobus sp.). C1
  • (b) Differentiate between Bacteria and Archaea based on cell wall structure, association of histone to DNA, and structure of membrane lipids. C3
  • (c) Describe the diversity of bacteria: cell shape (coccus, bacillus, spirillum, vibrio) and Gram stain (Gram-positive, Gram-negative). C2
  • (d) Explain the roles of bacteria: recycling of chemical elements (nitrogen fixation, Rhizobium sp.), symbiotic (E. coli in human intestine), pathogenic (Salmonella sp.), research and technology (bacterial plasmid). C3
Before you start

You need these ideas from earlier. Tap one if you need a reminder.

  • The plasma membrane controls what enters and leaves the cell. The cell wall lies outside it and gives shape and protection.

  • Membranes are made of phospholipids: a water-loving head with hydrocarbon (fatty-acid) tails. How the tails are joined to the head (ester or ether linkage) is one way Bacteria and Archaea differ.

  • In eukaryotes, DNA wraps around proteins called histones, which pack it into chromosomes.

  • Plants need nitrogen to make amino acids (for proteins) and nucleic acids (DNA, RNA), but they cannot use N₂ gas from the air directly.

The two prokaryote domains are Bacteria (E. coli) and Archaea (Sulfolobus sp.). Both are unicellular, with a single circular DNA in the nucleoid region. Some also carry extra circular DNA called plasmids.

Archaea were first found in extreme places. The three main types are methanogens, halophiles and thermoacidophiles.

Diversity of bacteria: Gram stain ​

Gram staining tells bacteria apart by their cell wall.

  • Cell wall: 1 layer, thick peptidoglycan
  • Colour: purple, crystal violet trapped
  • Danger: less pathogenic
  • Examples: Lactobacillus, Bacillus, Clostridium

Roles of bacteria ​

Recycling elements

Nitrogen fixation by Rhizobium: N₂ → NH₃, so plants can make amino acids and nucleic acids. Also act as decomposers.

Symbiotic

E. coli in the human intestine helps digest complex molecules like lactose.

Pathogenic

Release toxins (exotoxin, endotoxin). Salmonella causes food poisoning.

Research & technology

Bacterial plasmids in E. coli for gene cloning. Make cheese, yoghurt, soy sauce, vitamins, hormones and antibiotics.

Nitrogen fixation, step by stepN₂ in the air↓Rhizobium fixes N₂ → NH₃↓Plants use it to make amino acids and nucleic acids

1.4 Kingdom Protista ​

Learning outcomes
  • (a) State the unique characteristics of Protista. C1
  • (b) State the classification of Protista: two major phyla of algae by photosynthetic pigment (Chlorophyta, Chlamydomonas sp.; Phaeophyta, Fucus sp.) and four major phyla of protozoa by locomotion (Euglenophyta, Rhizopoda, Ciliophora, Apicomplexa). C1
  • (d) Explain the roles of Protista in the ecological community: CO₂ fixation, food source, eutrophication, red tide, human health, sewage treatment. C3
Before you start

You need these ideas from earlier. Tap one if you need a reminder.

  • Autotrophs make their own food, e.g. by photosynthesis. Heterotrophs get food from other organisms.

  • Pigments such as chlorophyll absorb light energy for photosynthesis. Different pigments absorb different colours of light, which is why algae can be grouped by pigment.

  • Asexual reproduction in which one cell divides into two identical cells.

  • Producers (e.g. algae) are eaten by consumers (e.g. zooplankton), passing energy along the chain.

Unique characteristics ​

  • Eukaryotes, mostly unicellular. Some are colonial, some are simple multicellular.
  • Nutrition can be photoautotrophic (algae), heterotrophic (most protozoa) or mixotrophic (Euglena).

Algae: grouped by photosynthetic pigment ​

Plant-like protists. They photosynthesise, have a cell wall and live in wet, low-light places.

Roles of Protista ​

CO₂ fixation

Phytoplankton are primary producers. They turn CO₂ into organic compounds by photosynthesis.

Food source

Chlorella feeds zooplankton in aquatic ecosystems.

Eutrophication

Extra nitrate and phosphate cause a green algae bloom. Oxygen drops and aquatic life dies.

Red tide

Bloom of dinoflagellates (Karenia brevis). Its toxin, brevetoxin, attacks the nervous system of fish.

Human health

Plasmodium causes malaria. Spread by the female Anopheles mosquito.

Sewage treatment

Protozoa and algae help clean wastewater so it is safe to release.

Eutrophication, step by stepExtra nitrate and phosphate↓Green algae bloom↓Oxygen drops↓Aquatic life dies

1.5 Kingdom Fungi ​

Learning outcomes
  • (a) State the unique characteristics of Fungi. C1
  • (b) State the classification of Fungi phyla based on the spore-bearing structure: Zygomycota (Rhizopus sp.), Ascomycota (Penicillium sp.), Basidiomycota (Agaricus sp.). C1
  • (c) Explain the roles of fungi: decomposers, symbionts, pathogens, food production (yeast), pharmaceutical (Penicillium). C3
Before you start

You need these ideas from earlier. Tap one if you need a reminder.

  • Starch is the storage carbohydrate of plants; glycogen is the storage carbohydrate of animals. Both are made of glucose.

  • Asexual: one parent, offspring genetically identical. Sexual: gametes from two parents fuse, so offspring vary.

  • Enzymes break large food molecules into small ones that can be absorbed. Fungi release them outside their body, then absorb the products.

  • Two different species living closely together. In mutualism both benefit; in parasitism one benefits and the host is harmed.

Unique characteristics ​

  • Eukaryotes. Mostly multicellular, but yeast is unicellular.
  • Cell wall made of chitin. Stores energy as glycogen. Non-motile.
  • Heterotrophic by absorption: they release enzymes, then absorb the food. They live as saprotrophs (on dead matter), parasites (on living hosts) or mutualists (both sides benefit).
  • Reproduce with spores, sexually or asexually.
  • Body is made of hyphae, which branch into a mycelium. Septate hyphae have cross-walls; coenocytic (aseptate) hyphae have none and hold many nuclei.

Classification by spore-bearing structure ​

Roles of fungi ​

Decomposers

Break down dead organisms and waste and absorb the nutrients.

Symbionts

Lichen = fungus + alga. Mycorrhizae = fungus + plant roots.

Pathogens

Humans: Candida, ringworm, athlete's foot. Plants: rusts and smuts on cereals (Puccinia graminis), chestnut blight.

Food production

Yeast (Saccharomyces) for bread, wine, beer and tapai.

Pharmaceutical

Penicillium produces penicillin.

1.6 Kingdom Plantae ​

Learning outcomes
  • 1.6 (a) Describe alternation of generation as the unique characteristic of Plantae. C2
  • 1.6 (b) State the classification of Plantae into four groups: bryophytes, pteridophytes, gymnosperms, angiosperms. C1
  • 1.6.1 Bryophytes: (a) describe unique characteristics C2; (b) state the three phyla, Hepatophyta (Marchantia), Bryophyta (Polytrichum), Anthocerophyta (Anthoceros) C1; (c) state the terrestrial adaptations C1.
  • 1.6.2 Pteridophytes: (a) describe unique characteristics C2; (b) state the two phyla, Lycophyta (Lycopodium, Selaginella) and Pterophyta (Dryopteris) C1.
  • 1.6.4 Gymnosperms: (a) describe unique characteristics C2; (b) state the four phyla, Cycadophyta (Cycas), Coniferophyta (Pinus), Ginkgophyta (Ginkgo biloba), Gnetophyta (Gnetum) C1.
  • 1.6.5 Angiosperms: (a) describe unique characteristics of Phylum Anthophyta. C2
  • 1.6.6 Explain the evolutionary relationships from bryophytes to angiosperms based on size, dominance, dependence, water for fertilisation, vascular tissue and embryo protection. C3
Before you start

You need these ideas from earlier. Tap one if you need a reminder.

  • A haploid cell has one set of chromosomes (n); a diploid cell has two sets (2n).

  • Mitosis keeps the chromosome number the same (n → n, 2n → 2n). Meiosis halves it (2n → n).

  • A male and a female gamete fuse (n + n) to form a diploid zygote (2n).

  • Vascular tissue. Xylem carries water and minerals; phloem carries sugars made in photosynthesis.

Unique characteristics ​

Multicellular eukaryotes that photosynthesise with chlorophyll, store starch, and have a cellulose cell wall. Their life cycle shows alternation of generations.

Alternation of generations ​

The life cycle switches between two multicellular stages that look different from each other:

  • Gametophyte (n, haploid) makes gametes by mitosis.
  • Sporophyte (2n, diploid) makes spores by meiosis.

What makes each group stand out ​

The most primitive land plants; they grow in damp, shady places. No true roots, stems or leaves; the body is a thallus held down by rhizoids. Gametes form in the antheridium (male, antherozoids) and the archegonium (female, oosphere).

Their terrestrial adaptations:

  • Waxy cuticle cuts water loss.
  • Multicellular gametangia: a jacket of sterile cells protects the gametes.
  • Multicellular, dependent embryo stays inside the female parent, which shields and feeds it.
  • Walled spores in a sporangium: sporopollenin makes the wall tough, so spores survive drying out in the air.

1.7 Kingdom Animalia ​

Learning outcomes
  • (a) Describe the unique characteristics of Kingdom Animalia. C2
  • (b) State the classification of Animalia into nine phyla: Porifera, Cnidaria, Platyhelminthes, Nematoda, Annelida, Arthropoda, Mollusca, Echinodermata, Chordata. C1
  • (c) Discover the unique characteristics of each phylum: Leucosolenia, Obelia, Taenia, Ascaris, Pheretima, Valanga, Achatina, Asterias, Amphioxus. C3
  • (d) Explain the evolutionary relationships of animals based on level of organisation, germ layers, body symmetry, body coelom and segmentation. C3
Before you start

You need these ideas from earlier. Tap one if you need a reminder.

  • A tissue is a group of similar cells doing one job; tissues work together as organs.

  • A fertilised egg divides into a ball of cells. These cells form germ layers, which later become all the body tissues. The first opening that forms in the embryo becomes either the mouth or the anus.

  • Closed: blood stays inside vessels. Open: blood leaves the vessels and bathes the organs directly.

  • A fluid-filled cavity that muscles squeeze against, giving the body support and shape, like a water balloon.

Unique characteristics ​

  • Multicellular eukaryotes with no cell wall; collagen gives support instead.
  • Heterotrophic (holozoic): they eat other organisms. Store energy as glycogen.
  • Cells form tissues (except the simplest animals). Most have nerves and muscles and are motile at some stage.
  • Mostly sexual: large non-motile eggs, small flagellated sperm. Adults are mostly diploid and go through embryonic development.

Arthropoda vs Chordata: key features ​

  • Tagmata (head, thorax, abdomen), so body regions can specialise.
  • Chitinous exoskeleton protects from water loss and injury and anchors muscles.
  • Paired jointed appendages for flexible movement.
  • Well-developed sense organs that pick up small changes fast.
  • Many respiratory organs: gills, trachea, book lungs.
  • Metamorphosis: larva and adult eat different food, so they compete less.
  • Many mouthpart types, so many diets.
