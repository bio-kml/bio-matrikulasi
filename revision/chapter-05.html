5.1 Aerobic Respiration ​

Learning outcomes
  • (a) State the needs for energy and the role of respiration in living organisms. C1
  • (b) Outline the complete oxidation of glucose which involves glycolysis, Krebs cycle and oxidative phosphorylation. C1
Before you start

You need these ideas from earlier. Tap one if you need a reminder.

  • Oxidation Is Loss (of electrons, often with hydrogen); Reduction Is Gain. The two always happen together: one molecule is oxidised while another is reduced.

  • NAD⁺ and FAD pick up hydrogen (electrons) from food molecules: NAD⁺ is reduced to NADH + H⁺, FAD is reduced to FADH₂. They carry the electrons to the electron transport chain.

  • Catabolic pathways break molecules down and release energy (respiration). Anabolic pathways build molecules and use energy.

  • Outer membrane, inner membrane folded into cristae, the intermembrane space between them, and the fluid matrix inside.

Big concept: redox in respiration

Respiration is one long chain of redox reactions. Glucose is oxidised (loses hydrogen and electrons) to CO₂; oxygen is reduced (gains them) to water. NAD⁺ and FAD carry the electrons in between.

C6H12O6⏟oxidised→ 6CO2+6O2⏟reduced→ 6H2O⟶6CO2+6H2O+energy (ATP+heat)

Cellular respiration: the catabolic pathways of aerobic and anaerobic respiration, which break down organic molecules and use an electron transport chain for the production of ATP.

Aerobic respiration: a type of respiration in which the organic compound (e.g. carbohydrate) is completely oxidised into carbon dioxide and water, with the release of energy (ATP), in a process requiring oxygen.

Where it all happens ​

ATP: what it is ​

  • ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is a universal form of energy: the common energy currency of the cell.
  • It consists of the sugar ribose, the nitrogenous base adenine and a chain of 3 phosphate groups.
  • The 3 phosphate groups are negatively charged and joined by 2 high-energy bonds; these unstable bonds can be broken by hydrolysis to drive endergonic reactions.
  • Hydrolysis of the end phosphate forms ADP + Pi and releases 31 kJ of energy per mole of ATP.

Why cells need energy ​

Cells harvest the chemical energy stored in organic molecules and use it to make ATP, which drives most cellular work:

Two ways to make ATP ​

An enzyme transfers a phosphate group from a substrate to ADP, forming ATP.

substrate−P+ADP⟶substrate+ATP

Happens in glycolysis (steps 7 and 10) and the Krebs cycle (step 5).

Quick reminder: phosphorylation

Phosphorylation: the addition of a phosphate group to an organic molecule.

The big picture ​

5.1.1 Glycolysis ​

Learning outcomes
  • Illustrate to explain glycolysis pathway: from glucose to pyruvate. C3
Before you start

You need these ideas from earlier. Tap one if you need a reminder.

  • Molecules with the same atoms (same formula) but a different arrangement, e.g. glucose and fructose.

  • The energy needed to start a reaction (Chapter 4). Glycolysis spends ATP first to make glucose reactive enough.

  • Enzyme names tell you the job: a kinase moves a phosphate to or from ATP; an isomerase makes an isomer; a dehydrogenase removes hydrogen (oxidises); a mutase moves a group within a molecule.

Glycolysis: a series of reactions that splits a glucose molecule (6C) into 2 molecules of pyruvate (3C).

  • Occurs in the cytosol / cytoplasm.
  • Can occur with or without O₂.
  • 10 steps, each needing a specific enzyme, in 2 phases: the energy investment phase (steps 1–5) and the energy payoff phase (steps 6–10).
  • ATP is made by substrate-level phosphorylation.

How to read the drawings

Each green circle is a carbon; each orange tag is a phosphate (P) on that carbon. Watch the carbons go from 6 to 3, and watch the phosphates go on and come off.

5.1.2 Pyruvate Oxidation ​

Learning outcomes
  • Describe the conversion of pyruvate to acetyl CoA. C2
Before you start

You need these ideas from earlier. Tap one if you need a reminder.

  • Removing a carboxyl group as CO₂. The molecule loses one carbon.

  • A coenzyme (Chapter 4) that carries 2-carbon acetyl groups into the Krebs cycle.

  • Moving substances across a membrane against a concentration gradient, using ATP.

The link between glycolysis and the Krebs cycle. On entering the matrix of the mitochondrion by active transport, pyruvate is converted to acetyl coenzyme A (acetyl CoA) by oxidative decarboxylation.

Pyruvate3C
NAD⁺CoApyruvate dehydrogenase complexCO₂NADH + H⁺
Acetyl CoA2C

  1. Decarboxylation

    A carboxyl group is removed from pyruvate as CO₂, leaving a two-carbon fragment.

  2. Oxidation

    The two-carbon fragment is oxidised by NAD⁺ to form an acetyl group; NAD⁺ is reduced to NADH + H⁺.

  3. Attachment of coenzyme A

    Coenzyme A is attached to the acetyl group, forming acetyl CoA.

Per glucose

This happens twice (2 pyruvate): 2 CO₂, 2 NADH + 2H⁺, 2 acetyl CoA, and no ATP.

5.1.3 Krebs Cycle ​

Learning outcomes
  • Illustrate to explain Krebs cycle. C3
Before you start

You need these ideas from earlier. Tap one if you need a reminder.

  • Two things in one step: the molecule is oxidised (NAD⁺ → NADH) and loses CO₂.

  • Another hydrogen carrier. FAD gains two hydrogens and is reduced to FADH₂.

  • The last product, oxaloacetate, is the starting molecule of the next turn, so the cycle keeps going as long as acetyl CoA arrives.

  • Also called the citric acid cycle; takes place in the matrix.
  • Makes 1 ATP per turn by substrate-level phosphorylation.
  • Most of the energy goes to NAD⁺ → NADH and FAD → FADH₂, which carry high-energy electrons to the electron transport chain.
  • 8 steps, each catalysed by a specific enzyme.

5.1.4 Oxidative Phosphorylation ​

Learning outcomes
  • (a) Illustrate to explain electron transport chain: the pathway of electron transport is NADH dehydrogenase, ubiquinone / CoQ, cytochrome c reductase, cytochrome c, cytochrome c oxidase. Include succinate dehydrogenase for FADH₂. C3
  • (b) Explain chemiosmosis: proton-motive force. C3
  • (c) Explain complete oxidation of one molecule of glucose in active cells to produce 38 ATP. C3
Before you start

You need these ideas from earlier. Tap one if you need a reminder.

  • Particles move from where they are more concentrated to where they are less concentrated. H⁺ does this through ATP synthase.

  • The last molecule in a chain to take the electrons. In aerobic respiration it is oxygen, which becomes water.

  • Each carrier in the chain is reduced when it takes electrons and oxidised when it passes them on. Every hand-over releases a little energy.

Oxidative phosphorylation: ATP is made as a result of the transfer of electrons from NADH or FADH₂ to O₂ by a series of electron carriers through redox reactions. It takes place on the inner membrane (cristae) and makes almost 90% of the ATP. Two parts: (i) the electron transport chain and (ii) chemiosmosis.

(i) Electron transport chain ​

Electron transport chain: a sequence of electron carrier molecules (membrane proteins) that shuttle electrons down a series of redox reactions that release energy used to make ATP.

Components: NADH dehydrogenase (complex I) · succinate dehydrogenase (complex II) · coenzyme Q / ubiquinone · cytochrome c reductase (complex III) · cytochrome c · cytochrome c oxidase (complex IV).

Remember the order (acronym)

"New Queens Read Cards Openly"NADH dehydrogenase → Q (ubiquinone) → cytochrome c Reductase → Cytochrome c → cytochrome c Oxidase → O₂ (FADH₂ joins at succinate dehydrogenase and goes straight to Q.)

  1. NADH gives up its electrons

    NADH (from glycolysis, pyruvate oxidation and the Krebs cycle) transfers its electrons to NADH dehydrogenase (complex I); NADH is oxidised to NAD⁺.

  2. FADH₂ gives up its electrons

    FADH₂ (from the Krebs cycle) transfers its electrons to succinate dehydrogenase (complex II); FADH₂ is oxidised to FAD.

  3. Electrons go to coenzyme Q

    Electrons from both are transferred to coenzyme Q.

  4. Down the chain by redox reactions

    From CoQ, electrons pass through cytochrome c reductase, cytochrome c and cytochrome c oxidase.

  5. Oxygen is the final electron acceptor

    O₂ combines with 2 electrons and 2 H⁺ to form water: 2H++2e−+12O2→H2O

(ii) Chemiosmosis ​

Chemiosmosis: an energy-coupling mechanism that uses energy stored in the form of a hydrogen ion gradient across a membrane to drive cellular work.

It refers to the flow of H⁺ from the intermembrane space to the matrix through ATP synthase.

Electrons moving along the ETC release free energy↓The energy pumps H⁺ from the matrix into the intermembrane space via 3 proton pumps: NADH dehydrogenase, cytochrome c reductase, cytochrome c oxidase↓H⁺ accumulates in the intermembrane space: a proton gradient, the proton-motive force↓H⁺ diffuses back into the matrix through ATP synthase, down its concentration gradient↓ATP synthase catalyses the phosphorylation of ADP to ATP

Common mistake

Complex II (succinate dehydrogenase) does not pump H⁺. Only complexes I, III and IV pump. That is why FADH₂ (which enters at complex II) gives fewer ATP than NADH.

5.2 Fermentation and Its Application ​

Learning outcomes
  • (a) Explain the process of lactate and alcohol fermentation. C3
  • (b) Describe the importance of fermentation in industry. C2
Before you start

You need these ideas from earlier. Tap one if you need a reminder.

  • Glycolysis step 6 needs NAD⁺. Without oxygen, NADH cannot be oxidised by the ETC, so NAD⁺ runs out unless it is regenerated another way.

  • When NADH gives its hydrogen to a molecule, NADH is oxidised back to NAD⁺ and the molecule is reduced.

Fermentation: a catabolic process that makes 2 ATP from glucose without an electron transport chain and produces ethanol or lactic acid.

  • In the absence of oxygen, many cells use fermentation to make ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation (glycolysis only).
  • Pyruvate (or its product) acts as the electron acceptor to oxidise NADH to NAD⁺.
  • The regenerated NAD⁺ is reused in glycolysis, so glycolysis can keep going.

Occurs in yeast and some bacteria, producing ethanol.

  3. Ethanal is reduced by NADH to ethanol; NADH is oxidised to NAD⁺.

Importance of fermentation in industry ​

Alcohol fermentationVinegar, beverages, alcohol

Yeast feeds on the sugar or starch of a plant liquid (fruits, whole grains, potatoes, rice), which ferments into alcohol.

Alcohol fermentationBakery

The CO₂ bubbles made by baker's yeast let bread rise.

Alcohol fermentationTapai

Made from white or glutinous rice, fermented by moulds, yeasts and bacteria.

Lactate fermentationCheese and yogurt

Lactic acid fermentation by certain fungi and bacteria in the dairy industry.

Lactate fermentationTempoyak

Durian flesh with salt or sugar. Lactic acid bacteria inhibit harmful decomposing bacteria such as E. coli, preserving it.

Lactate fermentationKimchi

Vegetables fermented by lactic acid bacteria; the lactic acid gives a sour flavour and raises acidity, making it safe to eat.

Aerobic respiration vs fermentation ​

Similarities: both involve glycolysis (glucose as the main respiratory substrate); both use NAD⁺ as an electron acceptor; both make ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation.
