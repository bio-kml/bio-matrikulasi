4.1 Properties of enzyme and mechanism of actions ​

Learning outcomes
  • (a) Describe the structure of enzyme as globular protein that can catalyse metabolic reaction.
  • (b) Describe the six classes of enzyme with example according to International Union of Biochemistry and Molecular Biology (IUBMB) classification.
  • (c) Explain how enzyme lowering activation energy.
  • (d) Illustrate to explain the mechanism of enzyme action based on induced fit model.
  • (e) Describe the factors that affect the enzymatic reaction: (i) substrate concentration; (ii) pH; (iii) temperature; (iv) enzyme concentration.
Before you start

You need these ideas from earlier. Tap one if you need a reminder.

  • Proteins are long chains of amino acids. Each chain folds into a specific 3D shape, and the shape decides what the protein can do.

  • Primary: the amino acid sequence. Secondary: α-helix or β-pleated sheet. Tertiary: the whole chain folded into a 3D shape, held by hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulphide bridges and hydrophobic interactions. Quaternary: two or more polypeptide chains together.

  • All the chemical reactions in a cell. Catabolic reactions break molecules down; anabolic reactions build them up.

  • Hydrolysis breaks a bond by adding water. Condensation joins two molecules and releases water.

Enzymes ​

Enzyme: a biological catalyst produced by living cells that speeds up the rate of chemical reaction by lowering the activation energy.

Structure of enzymes:

  • An enzyme is a globular protein and has tertiary or quaternary structure.
  • An enzyme has two sites:
    • Active site: the specific site (region) of an enzyme that binds to a specific substrate, in which catalysis occurs.
    • Allosteric site: a site on the enzyme other than the active site.

Six classes of enzyme (IUBMB) ​

Based on IUBMB, the name of an enzyme must end with "-ase", and enzymes are classified by the type of reaction they catalyse.

ClassWhat it catalysesExamples
Hydrolasesthe hydrolysis reaction: breakdown of a chemical bond of a complex substrate by addition of waterdigestive enzymes: maltase, sucrase, amylase
Isomerasesthe rearrangement of functional groups or atoms in a substrate molecule; convert one isomer to anotherisomerase, mutase
Transferasesthe transfer of a functional group from one substrate to anothertransaminase, hexokinase
Lyasesthe addition or removal of a functional group, or breakdown of a chemical bond of a complex substrate without addition of waterdecarboxylase
Oxidoreductasesthe redox reaction: transfer of oxygen and hydrogen atoms between substratesdehydrogenase, catalase
Ligasesthe formation of chemical bonds between two substrate molecules using ATP (energy)synthetase

Activation energy ​

Activation energy: the minimum amount of energy required by a reactant (substrate) to start a chemical reaction.

How an enzyme lowers activation energy:

  1. Acts as a template for substrate orientation

    The substrate binds to the active site. The enzyme brings substrate molecules closer to each other so they collide in the correct orientation, forming the enzyme-substrate complex.

  2. Stretches the substrate toward its transition state

    As the substrate binds, the enzyme may stretch the substrate molecules toward their transition state. This helps break existing bonds and form new bonds to make the product.

  3. Provides a favourable microenvironment

    The active site can create a microenvironment more suited to the reaction than the solution would be without the enzyme.

  4. Amino acids in the active site take part directly

    Sometimes there is brief covalent bonding between the substrate and the side chain of an amino acid in the active site. Later steps restore the side chains, so the active site is the same after the reaction as before.

Induced fit model ​

Proposed by Koshland (1959). The shape of the active site is flexible, not exactly complementary to the shape of the substrate. Step through it:

StepWhat happens
1The substrate binds to the active site; the binding induces a slight change in the conformation of the active site.
2The active site becomes fully complementary to the substrate, which fits precisely: enzyme-substrate complex; catalysis takes place.
3Product is formed; the active site changes back to its original conformation when the product is released.

Factors that affect the enzymatic reaction ​

Pick a factor and tap the graph:

  • Low substrate concentration: rate increases as substrate increases; not all active sites bind with substrate (some remain free).
  • Increasing substrate: more collisions between substrate and active sites, so more active sites bind with substrate, until there are no more free active sites.
  • Very high substrate: rate becomes constant; all active sites are bound (the active sites are saturated), so more substrate does not increase the rate.

Common mistake

A denatured enzyme is not "killed" (enzymes are not alive). Say the active site changes shape, so the substrate can no longer fit.

4.2 Cofactor ​

Learning outcomes
  • (a) Define cofactor. C1
  • (b) Explain the three types of cofactors and functions of: (i) metal ion activators (e.g. Mg²⁺); (ii) coenzyme (e.g. NAD); (iii) prosthetic group (e.g. haem group). C3
Before you start

You need these ideas from earlier. Tap one if you need a reminder.

  • Organic molecules are carbon-based, e.g. vitamins. Inorganic ions are not, e.g. Mg²⁺, Zn²⁺, Fe²⁺, Ca²⁺.

  • Organic substances needed in small amounts in the diet. Many coenzymes are made from vitamins.

  • In biology, a molecule that loses hydrogen is oxidised and one that gains hydrogen is reduced. Dehydrogenases remove hydrogen from their substrate.

  • The region of the enzyme that binds the substrate. If its shape is not right, the substrate cannot bind.

Cofactor: a nonprotein molecule or ion that is required for proper functioning of an enzyme.

It can bind tightly and permanently to the enzyme, or bind loosely and reversibly with the substrate.

Apoenzyme: inactive enzyme without its cofactor→+ cofactor→Holoenzyme: active enzyme with its cofactor

Three types of cofactor ​

  • Inorganic ions such as Ca²⁺, Zn²⁺, Mg²⁺, Fe²⁺.
  • Bind loosely and temporarily to the enzyme.
  • Change its active site to make the shape more suitable for a reaction to take place.
  • Necessary for the functioning of certain enzymes.

4.3 Inhibitors ​

Learning outcomes
  • (a) Define inhibitor. C1
  • (b) State the types of inhibitor: (i) competitive inhibitor; (ii) non-competitive inhibitor. C1
  • (c) Explain reversible and irreversible inhibition. C3
  • (d) Analyse graph related to competitive and non-competitive inhibition to the rate of enzyme activity. C3
Before you start

You need these ideas from earlier. Tap one if you need a reminder.

  • The active site binds the substrate. The allosteric site is another site on the enzyme; something binding there can change the shape of the active site.

  • Ionic bonds, hydrogen bonds and disulphide bridges hold the 3D shape. Breaking them changes the active site.

  • A chain of reactions, each with its own enzyme; the product of one reaction is the substrate of the next.

  • Cells make most of their ATP in respiration, in the electron transport chain, which passes electrons to oxygen at the end.

Inhibitor: a molecule that binds to an enzyme and reduces the rate of enzyme reaction.

  • It prevents the substrate from binding with the enzyme to form the enzyme-substrate complex.
  • The rate of reaction decreases.

Similarity: both inhibitors bind loosely and temporarily to the enzyme.

Inhibitor competes with the substrate for the active site↓Inhibitor binds to the active site↓Substrate cannot bind: no enzyme-substrate complex↓No product forms: rate of reaction is reduced

The substrate keeps using any unaffected enzyme, so the same quantity of product forms, but it takes longer.

Reversible and irreversible inhibition ​

Inhibition→Reversible: competitive or non-competitive→Irreversible
  • Inhibitors are bound loosely and temporarily; they form weak chemical bonds with the enzyme.
  • No permanent damage to the enzyme; the effect is temporary.
  • Removing the inhibitor restores the enzyme activity to normal.

Example: isoleucine (end-product inhibition)

Threonine deaminase (enzyme 1) starts the pathway that makes isoleucine from threonine↓Isoleucine (the end product) builds up to a high concentration↓Isoleucine binds to enzyme 1 and inhibits it↓The pathway stops, so there is no excess production of the end product

Common mistake

Increasing substrate concentration only helps against a competitive inhibitor. A non-competitive inhibitor does not compete for the active site, so more substrate does not reduce its effect.
