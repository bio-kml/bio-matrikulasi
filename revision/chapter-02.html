2.1 Ecosystem Concept ​

Learning outcomes
  • (a) Define ecosystem. C1
  • (b) Describe lake ecosystem based on: (i) light penetration (photic and aphotic/profundal); (ii) distance from shore (littoral, limnetic). C2
  • (c) Describe terrestrial ecosystem of tropical rainforest stratification (emergent, canopy, understory, shrub, ground layer/forest floor). C2
Before you start

You need these ideas from earlier. Tap one if you need a reminder.

  • Photosynthesis uses light energy to make glucose from CO₂ and water, releasing O₂. Respiration breaks glucose down to release energy, giving off CO₂. Both happen in plants; only respiration needs no light.

  • Producers (plants, algae) make their own food. Consumers eat other organisms. Decomposers (bacteria, fungi) break down dead matter.

  • A habitat is the place where an organism lives. A community is all the populations of different species living in one area.

What is an ecosystem? ​

Ecosystem: all the organisms in a given area as well as the abiotic factors with which they interact.

ComponentMeaningExamples
Bioticliving thingsplants, animals, decomposers
Abioticnon-living thingshydrosphere (water), atmosphere (air), lithosphere (soil and rock)
  • Biotic and abiotic components interact. This affects every community and the environment.
  • Abiotic factors limit the types of organisms that can survive in a habitat.
  • The interactions can be seen in energy flow (2.2) and nutrient cycling (2.3).

Lake ecosystem ​

A lake is divided into zones using two criteria: light penetration and distance from shore.

Based on light penetration ​

Photic zoneAphotic / profundal zone
Positionupper layer (surface waters)below the photic zone
Lightenough light for photosynthesisnot enough light for photosynthesis
Ratesrate of photosynthesis > rate of respirationrate of respiration > rate of photosynthesis
Organismsproducers (plants, phytoplankton) and consumersdetritivores that feed on dead organisms
Notesplit into littoral and limnetic zonesabsent in shallow lakes, because light reaches the bottom

Quick reminder: detritivore

A consumer that gets its energy and nutrients from non-living organic material, such as dead bodies, fallen leaves and wastes.

Compensation point ​

The boundary between the photic and aphotic zones is the (light) compensation point: the light intensity where the rate of photosynthesis is equal to the rate of respiration. Here, the CO₂ released by respiration equals the CO₂ used in photosynthesis.

Bright light near the surface: photosynthesis > respiration (photic zone)↓Light gets weaker with depth↓Compensation point: photosynthesis = respiration↓Dim or no light, deeper: respiration > photosynthesis (aphotic zone)

Based on distance from shore (the photic zone only) ​

Littoral zoneLimnetic zone
Positionwell-lit, shallow water near the shorewell-lit, open surface water far from the shore
Main producersrooted and floating aquatic plantsphytoplankton
Other organismsfloating plants (water lilies, duckweed), emergent and submerged plants, insect larvaezooplankton, fish
Littoral zoneRoles of aquatic plants
  • produce oxygen
  • food for animals
  • shelter for animals: insects, crustaceans, frogs and fish
Limnetic zoneRoles of phytoplankton
  • producer: the base of the food chain in the lake
  • produce oxygen

Benthic zone ​

  • The bottom of every aquatic zone, shallow (some light) or deep (no light).
  • Zone of decomposition: dead plants and animals sink, collect and become sediment.
  • Its energy comes from organic matter drifting down from the littoral and limnetic zones.
  • Oxygen-consuming organisms dominate. Its organisms are the benthos, e.g. decomposing bacteria and fungi.

Tropical rainforest ​

The richest and most diverse biome in structure and species. It has high temperature, high humidity and heavy rain all year. It is stratified into five layers:

Layer (top to bottom)FeaturesPlantsAnimals
Emergent layercrowns of the oldest, tallest trees, 40 m or more; direct sunlight, warmest, lowest humidity, strongest windTualang, Meranti, Cengaleagles, monkeys
Canopy30 to 35 m; receives much sunlightepiphytes, e.g. orchidshornbills, insects, bats
Understoryreceives little light; many plants climb up host treeslianas (woody vines), palmsbats
Shrub layersmaller plants; receives little lightferns (pteridophytes)tapir, deer
Ground layer / forest floordark and damp; dead matter is decomposed quickly by bacteria, fungi, insects and detritivores, so nutrients are recycled fastmosses (bryophytes), non-woody plantsants, centipedes

Quick reminder: epiphyte

A plant that makes its own food but grows on the surface of another plant, usually on branches or trunks, only for support.

2.2 Energy Flow Through Ecosystem ​

Learning outcomes
  • (a) State the type of ecological pyramids in relation to trophic level. C1
  • (b) Explain the energy transfer in ecological pyramids in relation to trophic level. C3
  • (c) Calculate energy loss in each trophic level. C3
Before you start

You need these ideas from earlier. Tap one if you need a reminder.

  • A food chain shows who eats whom, starting with a producer. The arrows show the direction energy flows: grass → grasshopper → frog → snake.

  • Energy is not made or destroyed, only converted: light → chemical energy (in food) → heat. Heat given off cannot be used again by living things.

  • Percentage = (part ÷ whole) × 100. For example, 2 out of 8 is (2 ÷ 8) × 100 = 25%.

Trophic level and ecological pyramids ​

Trophic level: the position (stage) that an organism occupies in a food chain.

A food chain usually has four or five trophic levels.

Type of pyramidWhat it shows at each trophic levelShape
Pyramid of numbernumber of individual organismsusually upright; can be inverted (e.g. one tree feeds many insects)
Pyramid of biomasstotal biomass of all organismsusually upright
Pyramid of energyenergy content of all organismsalways upright

Quick reminder: biomass

The total mass of organic matter in a group of organisms in a particular habitat, usually given as dry mass.

Energy transfer between trophic levels ​

  • Sun: the ultimate source of energy (light).
  • Producers convert only about 1% of the visible light that reaches them into chemical energy.
  • Primary consumers (herbivores) get about 10% of the energy in the producers; about 90% is lost.
  • Secondary consumers get about 10% of the energy in the herbivores; about 90% is lost.
  • Tertiary consumers get about 10% again.

Remember

Only about 10% of energy is transferred to the next trophic level. About 90% is lost at each transfer.

Why energy is reduced at each trophic level ​

Reason (starting from producers)ExamplePassed on to detritivores and decomposers?
not consumed (inedible)bones, plant rootsyes, after the organism dies
not digestedcellulose (leaves the body in faeces)yes
lost in excretion and egestionurine, faecesyes
lost in respirationlost as heatno: heat cannot be used by other organisms

Why a food chain has no more than five trophic levels ​

A large amount of energy is lost at each transfer, mostly as heat from respiration↓Each trophic level receives less energy than the level below it↓After the 5th trophic level, only a small amount of energy is left↓This energy is not enough to support another trophic level

So a shorter food chain loses less energy: more of the energy in the producers reaches the top consumer.

Calculating energy loss ​

Percentage of energy lost=initial energy−final energyinitial energy×100

Worked example

8 204 J is passed from 42 424 J in the producers to the next trophic level. Calculate the energy lost.

  1. Energy lost = 42 424 J − 8 204 J = 34 220 J
  2. Percentage lost = (34 220 J ÷ 42 424 J) × 100
  3. = 80.7% (so only 19.3% is transferred)

2.3 Biogeochemical Cycle ​

Learning outcomes
  • (a) State biogeochemical cycle components (cycling pool and reservoir pool). C1
  • (b) State examples of biogeochemical cycle (nitrogen cycle, carbon cycle, phosphorus cycle and sulphur cycle). C1
  • (c) Explain nitrogen cycle and carbon cycle. C3
Before you start

You need these ideas from earlier. Tap one if you need a reminder.

  • Organic compounds are carbon-based molecules made by living things, e.g. glucose, proteins. Inorganic substances include CO₂, water and mineral ions such as nitrate.

  • Plant roots absorb ions dissolved in soil water, e.g. nitrate (NO₃⁻), ammonium (NH₄⁺), phosphate (PO₄³⁻) and sulphate (SO₄²⁻).

  • Oxidation: gain of oxygen or loss of electrons. Reduction: loss of oxygen or gain of electrons.

  • Aerobic means with oxygen; anaerobic means without oxygen.

Biogeochemical cycle ​

Biogeochemical cycle: the cycling of chemical elements from the environment (abiotic) to organisms (biotic) and back to the environment.

Reservoir poolCycling pool
Componentabioticbiotic
Meaningpart of the Earth that stores the element (a storehouse)the element moving through organisms
Carbon exampleCO₂ gas in the atmospherecarbon in organic compounds of plants and animals

Four examples of biogeochemical cycles ​

CycleReservoir poolHow it enters the cycling pool
Nitrogen cycleatmosphere, as N₂ gas (about 80% of air)N₂ is fixed; plants absorb NH₄⁺ and NO₃⁻ from soil; animals eat plants or other animals
Carbon cycleatmosphere (CO₂); oceans (dissolved, as HCO₃⁻ and CO₃²⁻); limestone (CaCO₃); fossil fuels; soilphotosynthetic organisms fix CO₂ into organic compounds; these pass to animals, fungi, heterotrophic protists and prokaryotes
Phosphorus cyclesedimentary rocks of marine origin; phosphate (PO₄³⁻) in soil and dissolved in oceansplants absorb PO₄³⁻ from soil to make organic compounds; animals eat plants
Sulphur cyclemostly rocks; a small amount in the atmosphere as SO₂plants absorb sulphate (SO₄²⁻) from soil to make organic compounds; passed along the food chain

Sulphur

Nitrogen cycle ​

Nitrogen is cycled between abiotic and biotic components. About 80% of the atmosphere is N₂, but plants cannot use N₂ directly. The cycle has five major steps. Scroll through them:

1 / 5
Step 1

Nitrogen fixation

N₂ → NH₃ or NO₃⁻, which plants can use.

By atmospheric, industrial or biological fixation (see below).

Step 2

Nitrification

NH₃ / NH₄⁺ → NO₂⁻ → NO₃⁻ (oxidation).

Two kinds of nitrifying bacteria in soil: Nitrosomonas sp. or Nitrosococcus sp. (to NO₂⁻); Nitrobacter sp. (to NO₃⁻).

Step 3

Assimilation

Roots absorb NO₃⁻ and NH₄⁺; nitrogen is built into proteins, nucleic acids and chlorophyll.

Plants; animals assimilate nitrogen by eating plants.

Step 4

Ammonification

Nitrogen in wastes (urea in urine, uric acid in bird waste) and dead organisms → NH₃ / NH₄⁺, returned to soil and then nitrified again.

Decomposers: aerobic and anaerobic bacteria and fungi.

Step 5

Denitrification

NO₃⁻ → N₂ gas (reduction), returned to the atmosphere, closing the cycle.

Anaerobic denitrifying bacteria, e.g. Pseudomonas sp., Achromobacter sp., Bacillus sp.; they use NO₃⁻ as the electron acceptor in anaerobic respiration.

Scroll to continue

The cycle in one line

N₂ in the atmosphere (reservoir pool) → fixation → NH₃ / NH₄⁺ in soil → nitrification → NO₃⁻ in soil → assimilation → organic nitrogen in plants and animals → ammonification → NH₃ / NH₄⁺; denitrification returns NO₃⁻ to the air as N₂.

Quick reminder: anaerobic respiration

Respiration that uses an electron transport chain with a final electron acceptor other than oxygen, for example nitrate.

Three types of nitrogen fixation ​

Lightning: N₂ in the air is oxidised by O₂↓Nitrogen dioxide (NO₂) forms↓NO₂ dissolves in rain: 2NO₂ + H₂O → HNO₂ + HNO₃ (nitrous acid + nitric acid: acid rain)↓The acids react with compounds in soil, forming NO₃⁻

Quick reminder: symbiosis (mutualism)

Symbiosis is two species living in close contact. In mutualism, both species benefit, like legumes and Rhizobium.

Carbon cycle ​

Carbon is cycled between abiotic and biotic components. It is essential for proteins, nucleic acids, lipids and carbohydrates.

CO₂ in the atmosphere (reservoir pool)↓Carbon fixation (photosynthesis): organic compounds (e.g. carbohydrate) in producers such as plants and algae↓Feeding: organic compounds in consumers and decomposers (cycling pool)↓Respiration, decomposition, combustion, weathering: CO₂ returned to the atmosphere
6CO2+6H2O⟶C6H12O6+6O2
Process that returns CO₂What happens
Cellular respirationplants and animals use carbohydrate as fuel; CO₂ is released
Decompositionbacteria and fungi break down dead plants and animals; CO₂ is released
Plants buried under soil millions of years ago (marine organisms for oil)↓Form fossil fuels: coal, peat, oil↓Combustion of fossil fuels and wood↓CO₂ returned to the atmosphere

Fun fact

Peatlands cover only about 3% of the Earth's land, yet they store more than twice as much carbon as all the world's forests. Malaysia has large peat swamp forests. When peat is drained and burned, this stored carbon returns to the air as CO₂, and much of the haze in our region comes from burning peat.

UN Environment Programme (UNEP), 2022; Al Jazeera, Malaysia’s burning swamps and choking haze, 2016.

2.4 Conservation and Management ​

Learning outcomes
  • (a) Describe sustainable development. C2
  • (b) Explain threats to biodiversity in Malaysia. C3
  • (c) Describe conservation of diversity in Malaysia. C2
Before you start

You need these ideas from earlier. Tap one if you need a reminder.

  • The variety of life at three levels: genetic, species and ecosystem diversity.

  • Resources that can be replaced naturally, such as forests and fish, but only if we do not use them faster than they recover.

  • The place where an organism lives and finds food, shelter and mates. Losing the habitat means losing the species that depend on it.

Sustainable development ​

Sustainable development: development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.

Sustainable forestry

Retain seed (mother) trees; cutting limits; forest reserves; strip cutting; reforestation.

Sustainable agriculture

Biological control; crop rotation; row planting; contour farming; strip farming; terracing; control the use of pesticides and fertilisers.

Sustainable fishery

Open and closed fishing seasons; limit fleet size and days at sea; trawling in deep sea only; weight quota (limit the mass of fish caught); limit fishing areas; limit net size; aquaculture; e.g. the Tagal system in Sabah.

2.5 Population Ecology ​

Learning outcomes
  • (a) Explain biotic potential and environmental resistance and their effect on population growth. C3
  • (b) Explain carrying capacity and its importance. C3
  • (c) Describe natality and mortality and their effects on the rate of population growth. C2
  • (d) Explain population growth curves: (i) exponential growth curve: lag phase, log phase (human population); (ii) logistic growth curve: lag phase, log phase, decelerating phase, stationary phase (Paramecium sp. population). C3
  • (e) Explain the limiting factors affecting the population size: (i) density-dependent factors; (ii) density-independent factors. C3
Before you start

You need these ideas from earlier. Tap one if you need a reminder.

  • The x-axis is usually time; the y-axis is what changes (here, population size). A steeper curve means faster growth; a flat curve means no change.

  • In exponential growth the population multiplies by the same factor each time, e.g. doubling: 2 → 4 → 8 → 16 → 32. It starts slowly, then shoots up.

  • Competition: organisms need the same limited resource. Predation: one animal hunts and eats another. Parasitism: one organism lives on or in a host and harms it.

Population ecology: the study of interactions between organisms in a population and their environment.

Population growth is the change (increase or decrease) in population size.

Quick reminder: population

A group of individuals of one species living in the same general area.

Biotic potential and environmental resistance ​

Biotic potential (r): the maximum rate of increase in population size under optimal environmental conditions.

It depends on: the age when reproduction starts; the number of offspring each time; how often reproduction happens; and the survival rate of the offspring.

Environmental resistance: all the factors that prevent a population from growing continually to reach its biotic potential.

Biotic factorsAbiotic factors
Meaningrelationships between living organismsfactors of the non-living, physical environment
Examplespredation, competition, parasitism, diseaselimited food, limited water, light, limited space (overcrowding), shelter

Carrying capacity (K) ​

Carrying capacity (K): the maximum population size that a particular environment can support.

It depends on: the resources available (e.g. nutrients, water); the size of the population or community; and how much each individual uses. K is dynamic: it changes when the environment changes.

Zero environmental resistance↓Population grows exponentially at its full biotic potential

Importance of carrying capacity:

  • It keeps population size at equilibrium.
  • N < K: plenty of resources, growth increases.
  • N = K: growth slows to zero.
  • N > K: environmental resistance rises, growth decreases.
  • K rises: N can rise.

Natality and mortality ​

TermMeaning
Natality (birth rate)the rate at which individuals produce offspring
Mortality (death rate)the rate at which individuals die
Effect on populationResult
natality > mortalitypopulation size increases
natality < mortalitypopulation size decreases
natality = mortalitypopulation size is stable

Natality and mortality balance population growth, so the population oscillates around K: equilibrium.

Two basic forms: the exponential growth curve (e.g. human population) and the logistic growth curve (e.g. Paramecium sp. population). Switch between them and tap each phase:

Exponential (J-shaped)Logistic (sigmoid, S-shaped)

Phaseslag, loglag, log, decelerating, stationary
Limitunlimited growthlevels off at carrying capacity (K)

Limiting factors ​

Density-dependent factorsDensity-independent factors
Meaningtheir effect changes as population density changestheir effect does not depend on population size; often unexpected events
Examplescompetition; territorial behaviour; predation; disease and parasitism; accumulation of toxic wasteclimate or weather (killing frost, heatwave); natural disasters (flood, forest fire, tsunami); use of pesticides by humans

Quick reminder: population density

The number of individuals per unit area or per unit volume.

Density-dependent factors in action ​

Competition for resources (food, light, space) can be intraspecific (within the same species) or interspecific (between different species).

Population density increases; resources are limited↓Competition increases↓Population growth is limited

Grown alone, Paramecium aurelia and P. caudatum both show sigmoid growth. Grown together, P. aurelia is the better competitor and P. caudatum dies out.

Each individual defends a territory; neighbouring territories of one species barely overlap↓Number of territories is limited↓Individuals without a territory often fail to breed or die↓Population size is limited

Malaysian oil palm estates put up nest boxes for barn owls to control rats, a classic predator–prey limiting factor and a form of biological control. The Malaysian Palm Oil Board reports that one barn owl can eat up to two rats a day, while a snake eats only about one a week.
