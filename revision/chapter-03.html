3.1 Selection ​

Learning outcomes
  • (a) State the types of selection: (i) natural selection; and (ii) artificial selection. C1
  • (b) Define natural selection. C1
  • (c) State three types of natural selection: (i) stabilizing selection; (ii) disruptive selection; and (iii) directional selection. C1
  • (d) Analyze stabilizing selection, disruptive selection and directional selection with examples. C4
  • (e) Define artificial selection. C1
  • (f) Describe inbreeding and outbreeding. C2
Before you start

You need these ideas from earlier. Tap one if you need a reminder.

  • A gene is a section of DNA that codes for a characteristic. Alleles are different forms of the same gene, e.g. A and a.

  • Genotype: the alleles an organism has (e.g. Aa). Phenotype: the characteristic you can observe (e.g. beak size).

  • A dominant allele shows its effect with just one copy (AA or Aa). A recessive allele only shows when there are two copies (aa).

  • Individuals of the same species differ from each other. Much of this variation is inherited, so it can be passed on to offspring.

Two types of selection ​

Natural selection: a process in which individuals that have certain heritable traits survive and reproduce at a higher rate than other individuals because of those traits.

Artificial selection: the selective breeding of domesticated plants and animals to encourage the occurrence of desirable traits.

Natural selectionArtificial selection
Selecting agentEnvironmentHumans
TypesStabilizing, directional, disruptiveInbreeding, outbreeding
ResultIndividuals better suited to the environmentNew varieties useful to humans

Both change the allele and genotype frequencies of a population.

Quick reminder: key terms

Heritable trait: passed from parents to offspring through genes. Phenotype: an observable characteristic, e.g. beak size. Allele frequency: how common one allele is in a population.

Stabilizing selection: human birth weight ​

Babies of 3–4 kg have the highest survival↓Low-weight babies: much higher death rate↓High-weight babies: slightly higher death rate↓Selection acts against both extremes↓Intermediate weight is kept; curve becomes narrower

Directional selection: the peppered moth ​

Industrial melanism in Biston betulariaPollution darkens tree bark↓Light speckled moths are easily seen by predators↓Dark moths survive and reproduce more↓Dark form increases; curve shifts toward the dark extreme

Disruptive selection: seedcracker finch ​

Small beaks feed well on soft seeds↓Large beaks crack hard seeds↓Medium beaks are poor at both↓Medium-beaked birds have lower survival↓Curve splits into two peaks

Humans choose which organisms breed: selective breeding.

Quick reminder: homozygous, heterozygous, hybrid

Homozygous: AA or aa. Heterozygous: Aa. Hybrid: offspring of parents from different varieties, populations or species.

Inbreeding: benefits and problems ​

  • Keeps advantageous traits in the line
  • Uniform offspring
  • Predictable traits in offspring

Benefits of outbreeding ​

Hybrid vigour (heterosis)
Larger fruit, more fruit
Disease resistance
Earlier maturity
Better adaptation to environmental change
Better quality products, e.g. meat

3.2 Speciation ​

Learning outcomes
  • (a) Define biological species concept. C1
  • (b) Describe modes of speciation: sympatric and allopatric. C2
  • (c) State the processes that lead to speciation. C1
  • (d) Explain the processes that lead to speciation: (i) reproductive isolation; (ii) genetic drift; (iii) hybridization; and (iv) adaptive radiation. C3
Before you start

You need these ideas from earlier. Tap one if you need a reminder.

  • Individuals with useful heritable traits survive and reproduce more, so allele frequencies in the population change.

  • Body cells are diploid (2n), with two sets of chromosomes. Gametes are haploid (n), with one set. Fertilisation restores 2n.

  • A male and a female gamete fuse at fertilisation to form a zygote, which develops into the offspring.

  • A change in the DNA. It can create new alleles, which is one source of the variation that selection acts on.

Biological species concept ​

Species: a population or group of populations whose members have the potential to interbreed in nature and produce viable, fertile offspring, but not with members of other such groups.

Based on the ability to interbreed, not on looks.

CaseExampleWhy
Look alike, different speciesMeadowlarks Sturnella magna and S. neglectaBehaviour differs enough to stop interbreeding
Look different, same speciesAll humans (Homo sapiens)They can interbreed successfully

Quick reminder: viable and fertile

Viable: able to survive and develop. Fertile: able to reproduce.

Speciation ​

Speciation: the process of forming new species from pre-existing species through evolution.

One population linked by gene flow↓Barrier stops gene flow↓Allele frequencies change; groups diverge↓Reproductive isolation↓New species

If the two groups meet again:

  • They interbreed and have fertile offspring: no speciation
  • They cannot interbreed: speciation has occurred

Quick reminder: gene flow

The transfer of alleles from one population to another, by movement of fertile individuals or their gametes (e.g. pollen).

Modes of speciation ​

SympatricAllopatric
MeaningNew species form in the same geographical areaNew species form from geographically isolated populations

Quick reminder: ecological niche

The role of a species in its environment, including the resources it uses and the conditions it needs.

Sympatric speciation ​

Polyploidy: extra sets of chromosomes (Tragopogon).

T. dubius (2n = 12) × T. pratensis (2n = 12)→T. miscellus (2n = 24), new species
T. dubius (2n = 12) × T. porrifolius (2n = 12)→T. mirus (2n = 24), new species

Allopatric speciation ​

Geographical barrier splits population↓No gene flow↓Different selection pressures; allele and genotype frequencies change↓Genetic divergence↓Reproductive isolation↓New species

A barrier depends on how well the organism disperses. A canyon may stop small rodents, but not birds, coyotes or pollen.

Example: the Grand Canyon

  • North rim: white tail, dark belly (Kaibab squirrel)
  • South rim: grey tail, white belly (Abert squirrel)

Processes that lead to speciation ​

Four processes: reproductive isolation, genetic drift, hybridization and adaptive radiation.

(i) Reproductive isolation ​

Biological barriers that stop closely related species from interbreeding and producing viable, fertile offspring. They block gene flow between species.

Gametes↓Prezygotic barriers↓Zygote↓Postzygotic barriers↓Viable, fertile offspring
BarrierHow it worksExample

(ii) Genetic drift ​

A change in allele frequencies in a population due to chance, not natural selection.

  • Strongest in small populations
  • Reduces genetic variation
  • Shifts the population away from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium

Example: 10 wildflower plants

Generation 1: by chance, only 5 plants leave offspring↓Generation 2: by chance, only 2 plants (both CRCR) leave offspring↓Generation 3: all CRCR; CW allele lost

Quick reminder: gene pool and Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium

Gene pool: all the alleles in a population. Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium: allele and genotype frequencies stay the same over generations when no evolution happens.

Examples:

1814: 15 British colonists settle; one carries a recessive allele for retinitis pigmentosa↓Small, isolated gene pool↓Late 1960s: 4 of 240 descendants are blind↓Allele is 10 times more common than in the source population

(iii) Hybridization ​

A cross between two species usually gives a sterile F1 hybrid: its chromosomes are not homologous. Sometimes nondisjunction doubles the chromosomes. This allopolyploidy overcomes hybrid sterility and forms a new species.

Raphanus (2n = 18) × Brassica (2n = 18)↓Gametes n = 9 + n = 9↓Sterile F1 hybrid (18 non-homologous chromosomes)↓Nondisjunction doubles chromosomes↓Fertile Raphanobrassica (4n = 36): new species

Quick reminder: nondisjunction and allopolyploid

Nondisjunction: chromosomes fail to separate properly. Allopolyploid: has full chromosome sets from two different species.

(iv) Adaptive radiation ​

Adaptive radiation: a period of evolutionary change in which groups of organisms form many new species whose adaptations allow them to fill different ecological roles.

One ancestral species↓Spreads into many ecological niches↓Many new species, each adapted to one niche

They share homologous structures, modified for different functions.

Fun fact

The orangutans of Sabah and Sarawak are Bornean orangutans (Pongo pygmaeus). Those on Sumatra were named a separate species (Pongo abelii) in 2001. In 2017, scientists named a third species from Sumatra, the Tapanuli orangutan, after comparing skulls, behaviour and 37 genomes. It was the first new great ape species described since 1929. Fewer than 800 are left.

Nater et al., Current Biology (2017)
